Week 1 Lectures 1 – 3

Foundations of Interactive Systems, Usability & SDLCs

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Modern Age of Computers: Starts from 2011 onwards (Ubiquitous/Pervasive era). 1985 = Desktop GUI, 1998 = Web, 2007 = Mobile.
  • Generic User Categories: Novice, Intermittent, and Expert. ("Super knowledgeable" is NOT a generic category).
  • Usability 5 Components (Jakob Nielsen): Learnability, Efficiency, Memorability, Errors (low rate & easy recovery), Satisfaction. (Memorization is NOT one of them!).
  • Spiral Model 2nd Quadrant: Crucial for interactive software because it identifies and mitigates usability risks via prototyping.
  • Utility vs Usability: Utility = functional support for user needs (what it does); Usability = how easily and pleasantly users can use that functionality.

1. Definition of Computer & Interactive Systems

Oxford English Dictionary (OED) Definition: An electronic device capable of receiving information (data) in a particular form and performing a sequence of operations in accordance with a predetermined but variable set of procedural instructions (program) to produce a result in the form of information or signals.

Everyday Computing Examples:
  • Digital Pedometer: Differentiates walking from standing, counts steps at varying speeds, converts steps to distance using an internal formula stored in memory — fits the strict definition of a computer!
  • Microwave Ovens, ATMs, Smart TVs, Smartphones: All are interactive computing systems used by layman users (users who do not know the underlying computer technology).

2. Defining Usability

ACM SIGCHI

"Human-computer interaction is a discipline concerned with the design, evaluation and implementation of interactive computing systems for human use and with the study of major phenomena surrounding them."

ISO 9241-210:2009

"The extent to which a product can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction in a specified context of use."

Nielsen's 5 Quality Attributes Core Meaning for Interactive Design
LearnabilityHow easy is it for users to accomplish basic tasks the first time they encounter the interface?
EfficiencyOnce users have learned the design, how quickly can they perform tasks?
MemorabilityWhen users return to the design after a period of non-use, how easily can they re-establish proficiency?
ErrorsHow many errors do users make, how severe are these errors, and how easily can they recover?
SatisfactionHow pleasant is it to use the design? (Subjective user perception)

3. SDLC Models for Interactive Software

System-Centered Design (SCD)
  • Focuses on hardware/technology limits or what the programmer finds interesting/convenient to build.
  • User must adapt to system idiosyncrasies.
  • Leads to user anxiety, high error rates, and abandonment.
User-Centered Design (UCD)
  • Begins and ends with the user's mental models, workflows, and task contexts.
  • Iterative design-prototype-evaluate loop.
  • Layman users can operate system with minimal learning curve.
Boehm's Spiral Model — 4 Quadrants
Quadrant 1: Identify objectives, constraints & alternatives
Quadrant 2: Risk analysis, evaluation & risk mitigation via prototyping
Quadrant 3: Engineering, coding & testing of product
Quadrant 4: Customer evaluation & planning for next phase

Interactive systems require the iterative risk mitigation of Quadrant 2 to prevent developing interfaces that fail usability criteria.

Week 2 Lectures 4 – 6

Usability Requirements & Contextual Inquiry (CI)

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Contextual Inquiry: Is a semi-structured interview conducted while observing users perform tasks in their natural work setting.
  • CI Duration: Typically lasts 1 to 2 hours per session.
  • Two Modes: Active mode (ask questions during activity) and Passive mode (silent observation, questions afterward).
  • 5 Stages of Contextual Inquiry: Plan → Initiate → Execute → Reflect → Wrap-up.
  • Affinity Diagram: Involves 5 distinct steps for organizing qualitative observations.
  • Requirement Type: Usability requirements are Non-Functional Requirements (NFR).

1. Contextual Inquiry (CI) Core Principles

The motto of user-centric design is "Know thy user". Traditional requirements gathering often fails because users cannot articulate unconscious habits. CI bridges this gap by observing users in context.

1 Plan Stage

Define objectives, select user profiles, prepare recording materials, script procedures.

2 Initiate Stage

Contact authorities for permissions, establish initial rapport with participants.

3 Execute Stage

Observe user in their natural environment; probe breakdowns (active/passive inquiry).

4 Reflect Stage

Analyze gathered notes/recordings; identify user goals, pain points, and design targets.

5 Wrap-up Stage

Review findings with participants, validate interpretations, finalize documentation.

2. Affinity Diagram Method (5 Steps)

A technique to externalize and hierarchically structure large volumes of qualitative user observation notes:

  1. Step 1 — Brainstorming & Note Generation: Write down individual observations, user quotes, and observed breakdowns on separate sticky cards.
  2. Step 2 — Random Posting: Place all sticky cards randomly on a wall/board visible to the analysis team.
  3. Step 3 — Organic Clustering: Group cards naturally based on intrinsic affinity/similarity without imposing pre-existing preconceived categories.
  4. Step 4 — Naming Clusters: Create descriptive header labels (affinity cards) capturing the essence of each grouped cluster.
  5. Step 5 — Hierarchical Structuring & Review: Consolidate clusters into super-clusters and review for requirements synthesis.
Week 3 Lectures 7 – 13

SRS Specifications & Interface Design Guidelines

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • In SRS: Functions describe WHAT the system should do, NOT HOW to compute or implement the output!
  • Function Representation in SRS: Function Name, Input, Output, Description. (Motivation is NOT a standard function representation field).
  • Two Concerns in UCD: Interface Design (dialogs, look & feel) and Code Design (logic, data structures, algorithms).
  • DFD: A semi-formal code/system design language.
  • Design Levels: Identifying data structures and algorithms is done in the Detailed Design phase (not preliminary/high-level).
  • Apple Guidelines: Human Interface Guidelines for Apple/iOS/macOS are Specific design guidelines (not generic).
  • Shneiderman's 8 Golden Rules: "Interface should not force users to remember too many things" falls under Reduce short-term memory load.

1. Shneiderman's 8 Golden Rules of Interface Design

1Strive for Consistency: Consistent actions, terminology, layouts, color schemes, and fonts throughout the system.
2Cater to Universal Usability: Recognize diverse user needs (novices vs experts, accessibility for elderly or disabled users).
3Offer Informative Feedback: For every user action, provide immediate visual/auditory confirmation.
4Design Dialogs to Yield Closure: Sequences of actions should have clear beginning, middle, and completion states.
5Prevent Errors: Design UI so users cannot make serious errors (disable invalid buttons, provide input masks).
6Permit Easy Reversal of Actions: Explicit "Undo" functionality relieves anxiety and encourages exploratory learning.
7Support Internal Locus of Control: Experienced users desire the sense that they are in charge of the system, not vice versa.
8Reduce Short-Term Memory Load: Humans can only retain 7 ± 2 chunks of information in working memory; rely on recognition rather than recall.
Week 4 Lectures 14 – 17

Norman's Interaction Model & Prototyping

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Norman's Model: User behavior is represented as a series of actions across two primary stages: Execution Stage and Evaluation Stage.
  • Gulf of Execution: The gap between the user's intentions and the actions supported/allowed by the physical interface.
  • Gulf of Evaluation: The gap between the system's physical state presentation and the user's perception/understanding. ("Perceive system state" is in the Evaluation Stage).
  • Slips vs Mistakes: Slips = right goal, faulty execution; Mistakes = wrong goal formulated due to incorrect mental model.
  • Horizontal vs Vertical Prototype: Vertical prototype implements a narrow set of features in deep detail with back-end functionality. (A prototype depicting the entire interface at surface level without functionality is Horizontal!).
  • Wizard of Oz: Subject believes they interact with an autonomous computer, but it is operated behind the scenes by an unseen human.
  • Paper Mock-ups: Prime example of Low-fidelity prototype.

1. Don Norman's 7 Stages of Action

0. FORMING THE GOAL (State what is to be achieved)
Execution Stage (Form → Act)
  1. 1. Form the Intention: Decision to act to achieve goal.
  2. 2. Specify the Action: Translate intention into interface commands.
  3. 3. Execute the Action: Physical interaction (click, type, gesture).
Gap here = Gulf of Execution
Evaluation Stage (Sense → Assess)
  1. 4. Perceive System State: Observe screen changes/feedback.
  2. 5. Interpret System State: Assign meaning to visual output.
  3. 6. Evaluate Outcome: Compare interpreted state to initial goal.
Gap here = Gulf of Evaluation

2. Prototyping Dimensions

Fidelity Level Characteristics When to Use
Low Fidelity (Lo-Fi) Paper mockups, sketches, index cards. Very cheap, fast, easily discarded. Early design exploration; encourages candid user feedback and radical redesigns.
Medium Fidelity Clickable wireframes, digital storyboards, interactive mockups with simulated data. Evaluating workflow sequences, navigation flow, and screen transitions.
High Fidelity (Hi-Fi) Fully coded UI, realistic aesthetics, interactive components. Expensive & time-consuming. Late-stage testing; measuring precise task completion times, latency, and detailed UI usability.
Week 5 Lectures 15 – 17

Rapid Prototype Evaluation Techniques

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • How to evaluate an interface? By evaluating its usability (which encompasses learnability, efficiency, satisfaction).
  • Cognitive Walkthrough: Requires a team of 3 to 5 evaluators. Evaluators answer questions in detailed qualitative rationale (NOT just "yes" or "no"!).
  • Heuristic Evaluation: Evaluators inspect the UI against a checklist of usability principles called heuristics. (Typically 3-5 evaluators catch ~75% of bugs).
  • Valid Prototype Evaluation Methods: Cognitive Walkthrough and Heuristic Evaluation. (Affinity Diagram is for requirement clustering, NOT prototype evaluation!).
  • SUS (System Usability Scale): A 10-item Likert questionnaire. A score of 70% (or ~68) or higher is considered desirable/acceptable.
  • ASQ (After-Scenario Questionnaire): A post-task questionnaire (administered immediately after completing a specific scenario).

1. Cognitive Walkthrough (CW) 4 Guiding Questions

A task-centric inspection method focused on first-time/novice learnability:

Q1: Will the user try to achieve the right effect? (Does user's intention match system expectation?)
Q2: Will the user notice that the correct action is available? (Is the button/control clearly visible?)
Q3: Will the user associate the correct action with the desired effect? (Is label/icon meaningful?)
Q4: If the correct action is performed, will the user see that progress is being made? (Is feedback clear?)

2. Standard Usability Survey Instruments

ASQ (After-Scenario Questionnaire)
  • Type: Post-task questionnaire.
  • 3 brief questions assessing: ease of completing task, satisfaction with time taken, satisfaction with support information.
  • Administered immediately after each task scenario.
SUS (System Usability Scale)
  • Type: Post-session standardized questionnaire.
  • 10 alternating positive and negative statements on a 5-point Likert scale.
  • Yields normalized score from 0 to 100. Desirable benchmark: ≥ 68 – 70 (Above average / Grade C+ or B).
Week 6 Lectures 18 – 20

System Design: Cohesion, Coupling, DFD & ER Diagrams

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Characteristics of Good Design: Coverage, Correctness, Understandability, Efficiency. ("Colorful" is NOT a characteristic!).
  • Cohesion: Pertains to relationships within a single module (aim for High Cohesion).
  • Coupling: Pertains to relationships between modules (aim for Low Coupling).
  • Logical Cohesion: When all functions perform logically similar operations (e.g., all input routines).
  • Content Coupling: Occurs when two modules share code or direct internal data — the worst form of coupling!
  • ER Diagram Components: Entity, Attribute, and Relationship.
  • Null Attributes: Used when an entity does not have a value for an attribute.
  • 4 ER Relationship Types: One-to-One, One-to-Many, Many-to-One, Many-to-Many.
  • DFD: Data Flow Diagram.

1. Cohesion Hierarchy (Highest/Best to Lowest/Worst)

Week 7 Lectures 21 – 23

Object-Oriented Design (OOD) & UML Modeling

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Functional vs OOD: In functional approach, system is seen as services (functions). In OOD, system is seen as interacting objects containing data and behavior.
  • Class Anatomy: Contains attributes (data nature/state) and operations (methods denoting class behavior).
  • UML Nature: UML is a modeling language to represent OOD — it is NOT a software development methodology!
  • Implementation View: Captured using the Component Diagram. (Hardware/physical topology = Deployment diagram).
  • Entity Objects: Stereotype objects that hold information/data (e.g. Book, Student, Register).

1. Object Stereotypes in Analysis & Design (BCE Pattern)

🔲
Boundary Objects

Handle interaction between the system and its actors (users or external hardware). Represent UI screens, dialogs, sensors, and network interfaces.

⚙️
Controller Objects

Orchestrate workflows, business rules, and use-case execution logic. Coordinate communication between boundary and entity objects.

📦
Entity Objects

Hold persistent domain data, business concepts, and state (e.g., Customer, Account, Invoice, Course). Remain stable over long periods.

Week 8 Lectures 24 – 26

Coding Guidelines & Review-Based Code Testing

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Coding Guidelines: Standard conventions improve readability, understandability, and teamwork.
  • Function Length: Functions should ideally be short — no longer than 10 lines of code (or 10-20 max).
  • Things to Avoid in Code: Lengthy functions, using the same identifier for multiple purposes, and indiscriminate use of 'goto' branching statements.
  • Two Code Testing Categories: Review-based (static) and Execution-based (dynamic).
  • Two Review-Based Methods: Code Inspection and Code Walkthrough.
  • Code Walkthrough: Requires 3 to 5 members; involves simulating code execution by hand (dry run) with test inputs.
  • Uninitialized Variables: Classic programming error caught reliably during formal code inspection.

1. Review-Based Testing Comparison

Code Walkthrough (Informal Simulation)
  • Team size: 3 – 5 members.
  • Author leads the review team through the code logic.
  • Team dry-runs the code with test data by hand.
  • Focuses on checking algorithmic correctness and structural flow.
Code Inspection (Formal Checklist)
  • Formal defect discovery process with assigned roles (Moderator, Inspector, Reader, Scribe).
  • Inspectors check code against an established defect checklist.
  • Catches uninitialized variables, out-of-bounds array access, memory leaks, resource leaks, non-terminating loops.
Week 9 Lectures 27 – 28

Black-Box Testing (Functional Testing)

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Black-Box Testing: Designs test cases based solely on inputs and outputs without any knowledge of internal code structure.
  • Test Suite: The complete set of all test cases with which a program is tested. (Randomly chosen test cases do not ensure significance!).
  • Two Main Approaches: Equivalence Class Partitioning (ECP) and Boundary Value Analysis (BVA).
  • ECP Core Theorem: Testing with any ONE value from an equivalence class is as good as testing with ALL input values belonging to that class!

1. Equivalence Class Partitioning (ECP)

The input domain is partitioned into sets called Equivalence Classes:

  • Valid Equivalence Classes: Valid inputs satisfying program specifications.
  • Invalid Equivalence Classes: Erroneous, out-of-range, or unexpected inputs that the system should reject gracefully.

2. Boundary Value Analysis (BVA)

Experience demonstrates that software errors cluster overwhelmingly at the boundaries of input ranges. For an input interval $[a, b]$, BVA tests:

a − 1 (Invalid) a (Min) a + 1 (Just above min) Nominal (Typical) b − 1 (Just below max) b (Max) b + 1 (Invalid)
Week 10 Lectures 29 – 30

White-Box Testing, CFG & System Integration

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • White-Box Testing: Requires thorough knowledge of the internal structure and logic of the code.
  • Control Flow Graph (CFG): To construct CFG, number all statements, create a node for each numbered statement, and add directed edges for transfers of control.
  • CRITICAL COVERAGE RULE: Branch coverage GUARANTEES statement coverage (Statement coverage does NOT guarantee branch coverage!).
  • McCabe's Cyclomatic Complexity Formula: V(G) = E − N + 2 (where E = number of edges, N = number of nodes). Alternatively V(G) = P + 1 where P = predicate nodes.
  • 3 Main Stages of System Testing: Alpha testing → Beta testing → Acceptance testing.
  • Volume Testing: Tests system performance under very large input datasets.

1. Coverage Criteria & McCabe's Metric

McCabe's Cyclomatic Complexity
V(G) = E − N + 2 = P + 1

Represents the number of linearly independent execution paths through the program. If $V(G) le 10$, the code is modular and testable; $> 20$ indicates high risk and complexity.

Coverage TypeDefinitionRelative Strength
Statement CoverageEvery executable code statement is traversed at least once.Weakest structural criterion
Branch / Decision CoverageEvery conditional branch (True and False edge) is taken at least once.Guarantees Statement Coverage!
Path CoverageAll linearly independent paths through the CFG are executed.Strongest (often intractable)

2. System Testing Stages

α Alpha Testing

Conducted by internal developers/testers at the developer's site in a controlled simulated environment.

β Beta Testing

Conducted by external prospective end-users in real operating field environments without developer control.

✓ Acceptance Testing

Conducted by the customer/client to decide whether to formally accept and pay for the delivered system.

Week 11 Lectures 31 – 34

Empirical Studies & Experiment Design

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Empirical Research: Controlled experiment to collect and analyze data on user behavior based on observation (empirical data).
  • 4 Stages of Empirical Research: 1. Research Question, 2. Variable Determination, 3. Experiment Design, 4. Data Analysis.
  • Research Questions: RQ1 "Is technique good?" is non-testable; RQ3 "Does interface enter text faster than Word?" is testable.
  • Null Hypothesis ($H_0$): States that the test condition will NOT affect the outcome (no difference).
  • Confounding Variables: Variables that influence both the independent and dependent variables, potentially leading to incorrect causal conclusions.
  • Thermometer Data: Recording temperature from a thermometer is an example of Interval scale data (arbitrary zero point).
  • Sample Sizes: Pilot study = 5 participants; Reliable conclusions = 12 to 25 participants.
  • Between-Subject Study: Different participants test different experimental conditions. (Within-subject = same participant tests all conditions).

1. Measurement Scales (Stevens' Taxonomy)

ScalePropertiesExamples in HCI
NominalArbitrary codes, naming, category labels (no order).Browser type, Gender, OS preference, User ID.
OrdinalOrdered ranks, but intervals between ranks are unequal.Likert scale rankings (1=Poor to 5=Excellent), race placement.
IntervalEqual intervals between values, but arbitrary zero.Celsius/Fahrenheit temperature, calendar dates.
RatioEqual intervals with an absolute, meaningful true zero.Task completion time (seconds), error count, typing WPM.
Week 12 Lectures 35 – 38

Data Analysis, COCOMO & Agile Development

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Significance Test: With significance testing, we try to REFUTE / REJECT the null hypothesis ($H_0$).
  • Type I Error: Also known as a False Positive (rejecting a true null hypothesis). Type II = False Negative.
  • Two Categories of Significance Tests: Parametric (e.g. t-test, ANOVA) and Non-parametric (e.g. Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon). t-statistic is NOT the only way!
  • Project Planning 4 Dimensions: Estimation of Project Size, Cost, Duration, and Effort.
  • COCOMO: Constructive Cost Estimation Model (developed by Barry Boehm). Boehm recommends estimation through 3 stages: Basic, Intermediate, and Detailed.
  • Gantt Chart: Horizontal bar chart where the length of each bar is proportional to the duration planned for that activity.
  • Agile / Scrum: Agile ensures quick turn-around of projects; Scrum is an Agile method.

2. Boehm's COCOMO Hierarchy

Basic COCOMO

Single-variable model estimating effort purely as a non-linear function of delivered lines of code: Effort = a × (KLOC)^b.

Intermediate COCOMO

Applies 15 Cost Drivers (product attributes, hardware constraints, personnel experience) to tune the nominal effort estimate.

Week 1 Intro & Foundations

Introduction to OB, Scientific Management & Hawthorne Studies

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Definition of Behaviour: Response of the organism/system to various stimuli (internal/external, conscious/subconscious, overt/covert, voluntary/involuntary).
  • Organization: Social arrangement of individuals with defined roles and goals. UNICEF, TATA Steels, Facebook are organizations; Germany is a nation-state, NOT an organization!
  • Efficiency: More output for less input / optimal output for given input.
  • Mother of OB: Industrial Psychology.
  • Pioneers: Scientific Management = Frederick W. Taylor (Time and Motion Study); Assembly Line = Henry Ford.
  • Control Group: The experimental group in which no changes are made (used as comparison baseline).
  • Hawthorne Studies: Conducted by Elton Mayo at Western Electric to study physical conditions (illumination). Revealed that worker productivity improved primarily due to social factors and feeling valued, not light levels!

1. The Foundations of Organizational Behaviour

Organizational Behaviour (OB): The systematic study of the actions and attitudes that people exhibit within organizations, analyzed across three distinct levels:

1. Individual Level

Perception, learning, personality, values, attitudes, emotions, and motivation.

2. Group Level

Group dynamics, team development, communication, leadership, power, conflict, and decision making.

3. Organizational Level

Organizational culture, structure, work climate, and change management.

Week 2 Perception & Attribution

Perception, Gestalt Principles & Attribution Theory

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Perception Nature: Perception is highly subjective (it is NOT objective!). Defined as giving meaning to sensory stimuli through organizing and interpreting.
  • Internal Factors of Perceptual Selection: Habit, motives, needs, interests. (Contrast, motion, repetition are external factors).
  • Gestalt Principles: Proximity (dots close together seen as columns), Similarity, Continuity, Closure, Symmetry.
  • Perceptual Constancy: Perceiving an opening door as a rectangle even as retinal projection turns trapezoidal.
  • Kelley's Causal Attribution:
    • Consensus: Extent to which other people behave the same way in this situation.
    • Consistency: Extent to which the person behaves the same way over time.
    • Distinctiveness: Extent to which the person behaves differently in other situations.
  • Fundamental Attribution Error: Tendency to attribute others' actions to internal causes (personality/laziness) while largely ignoring external situational factors.
  • Glass-Ceiling Effect: Few women reaching top management despite many at bottom/middle roles.

1. Attribution Theory (Harold Kelley's Covariation Model)

Attribution DimensionHigh LevelLow LevelResulting Attribution
Consensus (Others do it?) Everyone arrives late today. Only John arrives late today. High Consensus + High Distinctiveness + Low Consistency = External / Situational Attribution.

Low Consensus + Low Distinctiveness + High Consistency = Internal / Dispositional Attribution.
Distinctiveness (Unique to this task?) John is only late for this meeting. John is late for all kinds of events.
Consistency (Always happens over time?) John is late every single week. John is almost never late.
Week 3 Personality & Johari

Personality Theories, Big Five & Johari Window

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Etymology: Personality comes from Latin 'Persona', meaning Mask.
  • Johari's Window: Part unknown to ourselves but known to others is the Blind Self.
  • Sigmund Freud: Deepest level of consciousness is the Unconscious. Reality principle is operated by the Ego. (Id = Pleasure principle, Super-Ego = Moral principle).

  • Self Theory: Main proponent was Carl Rogers.
  • Self-Esteem: Employees with high self-esteem are more ready to take responsibilities.

3. HIDDEN SELF: Known to self & Unknown to others (Facade / Secrets)
4. UNKNOWN SELF: Unknown to self & Unknown to others (Unconscious potential)

Week 4 Learning & Attitudes

Learning Models, Conditioning & Attitudes

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Attitudes: Evaluative statements towards objects, people, or events. Providing more information CAN lead to change in attitude.
  • Learning Definition: Relatively permanent change in behaviour occurring as a result of experience.
  • Pavlov's Experiment: The food presented which makes dog salivate naturally is the Unconditioned Stimulus (UCS).
  • Extinction in Operant Conditioning: The process through which responses that are no longer reinforced tend to gradually diminish in strength.
  • Negative Reinforcement: Withdrawal of an unpleasant stimulus to strengthen a desired behaviour. (Punishment adds unpleasant stimulus to decrease behaviour).
  • Observational Learning: Kid copying dance moves after watching TV is an example of Observational Learning / Modeling (Bandura).
  • Opinion: Opinion is primarily Cognitive in nature.
  • Valence: The intensity or degree of favorableness/unfavorableness of an attitude.
  • Cognitive Dissonance: Uncomfortable tension from holding two conflicting thoughts at the same time (Leon Festinger).
  • Social Desirability Effect: Giving answers perceived as socially acceptable rather than completely honest.

1. Operant Conditioning Matrix (B.F. Skinner)

ActionStimulus Presented (+)Stimulus Withdrawn (−)
Behavior Strengthened (↑)Positive Reinforcement: Give reward for good performance.Negative Reinforcement: Stop annoying alarm when seatbelt fastened.
Behavior Weakened (↓)Positive Punishment: Issue reprimand for coming late.Extinction: Stop praising; ignore unwanted behavior until it ceases.
Week 5 Emotions & Stress

Emotions, Emotional Labor & Stress at Workplace

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Eustress: The positive stress resulting from positive, exhilarating events. (Distress is harmful stress).
  • Mood: An unfocused, relatively mild feeling that exists as background to our daily experience.
  • Emotional Labor: The psychological effort involved in holding back true emotions to display organizationally required emotions.
  • Mood Congruence Effect: When John fails his driving test and immediately starts recalling his friends teasing him about driving.
  • Frustration-Based Aggression: Aggression triggered when you have a goal and feel someone/something is blocking you from reaching it.
  • Resentment: Defined as a mild form of hate.
  • Burnout: Complete exhaustion accompanied by perceived lack of meaning resulting from prolonged chronic stress.
  • Non-Assertiveness: Fear of being rejected is one of the main causes.

1. Emotions vs Moods

Emotions
  • Caused by a specific, identifiable event or person.
  • Very brief in duration (seconds to minutes).
  • Specific and numerous in nature (anger, fear, joy, disgust).
  • Accompanied by distinct facial expressions and action readiness.
Moods
  • Cause is often general, unclear, or diffuse.
  • Longer lasting (hours to several days).
  • More general: positive affect vs negative affect.
  • Generally not indicated by distinct facial expressions.

▼

Week 8 Communication

Communication Systems & Information Sharing Traps

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Encoding: The process of converting an intended message into communicable symbols/language.
  • Noise: Any disturbance that interferes with transmission, receipt, or feedback of a message.
  • Communication Climate: Level of openness in an organization that gives members comfort.

1Driver: Decisive, fast-paced, task-oriented, direct, and pragmatic.
2Expressive / Animated: Enthusiastic, persuasive, relies on feelings and intuition, high energy.
3Amiable: Patient, supportive, collaborative, seeks harmony, avoids confrontation and rapid change.
4Analytical: Systematic, data-oriented, cautious, logical, methodical, detail-focused.
Week 9 Decision Making

Decision Making Approaches & Cognitive Biases

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Orientation Step: The step in team decision making where the problem is defined and consensus reached.
  • Satisficing Approach (Herbert Simon): Finding a solution that is satisfactory and sufficient rather than optimal.
  • Incremental Approach: Making the smallest response possible that reduces a problem to a tolerable level.
  • Buck Passing: Shifting responsibility for decision making onto somebody else.
  • Anchoring and Adjustment: Tendency to be disproportionately influenced by an initial figure/anchor.
  • Groupthink: Group emphasis on harmony and conformity leading to poor, uncritical decisions.
  • Sunk Costs: CANNOT be recovered! Tendency to throw good money after bad is Escalation of Commitment.
  • Framing: Tendency to make different decisions depending on how a problem is worded/presented.

1. Classical Decision Traps & Group Pitfalls

Groupthink (Irving Janis)

Pressure for consensus overrides realistic appraisal of alternative courses of action. Illusion of invulnerability and self-censorship.

Abilene Paradox

A group collectively decides on a course of action that none of the individual members privately wanted, because everyone mistakenly assumed others wanted it.

Escalation of Commitment

Pouring further time and resources into an obviously failing endeavor due to psychological attachment to unrecoverable sunk costs.

Week 11 Group Dynamics

Group Dynamics, Tuckman Stages & Social Loafing

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • Group vs Team: A team has shared mutual accountability, common purpose, and complementary skills.
  • Tuckman's 5 Stages: Forming → Storming → Norming → Performing → Adjourning.
  • Social Loafing (Ringelmann Effect): Tendency for individuals to expend less effort when working collectively in a group than when working alone.
  • Countering Social Loafing: Make individual contributions identifiable, assign unique meaningful tasks, maintain smaller group sizes.
  • Norms: Acceptable standards of behavior shared by group members (e.g., Hawthorne bank wiring room restriction of output).

1. Tuckman's 5 Stages of Group Development

1 Forming

Orientation, ambiguity about purpose, testing boundaries, polite behavior.

2 Storming

Intragroup conflict, jockeying for leadership and roles, resistance to group constraints.

3 Norming

Cohesion develops, standard norms and common expectations established.

4 Performing

Structure is fully functional; team energy channeled into high task execution.

5 Adjourning

Wrapping up activities, preparing for disbandment, reflection.

Week 12 Culture & Change

Organizational Culture & Kurt Lewin's Change Model

▼
🎯
Exam Must-Knows & Traps:
  • System: A set of interrelated and interdependent parts arranged in a manner that produces a unified whole.
  • Edgar Schein's 3 Levels of Culture: Artifacts (surface visible), Espoused Values (stated strategies/goals), and Underlying Basic Assumptions (unconscious beliefs).
  • Kurt Lewin's 3-Step Change Model: Unfreezing → Moving / Changing → Refreezing.
  • Unfreezing: Overcoming individual resistance and group conformity to create readiness for change.
  • Refreezing: Stabilizing and institutionalizing a change intervention by balancing driving and restraining forces.

1. Edgar Schein's Three Levels of Culture

1. Artifacts (Surface)

Visible products, dress codes, office architecture, published lists of values, rituals, myths, and language.

2. Espoused Values (Intermediate)

Consciously stated goals, strategies, and philosophies (what the leadership formally proclaims as company values).

3. Basic Assumptions (Deep Core)

Unconscious, taken-for-granted beliefs, perceptions, and thoughts that form the ultimate source of organizational values and actions.

2. Kurt Lewin's 3-Step Model of Change

❄️ Unfreezing

Create motivation to change, challenge existing assumptions, dismantle psychological defense mechanisms.

🔄 Moving / Changing

Learn new concepts, adopt new behavioral models, implement revised structural processes.

🧊 Refreezing

Integrate changes into daily routines, reward desired behaviors, establish new cultural equilibrium.
